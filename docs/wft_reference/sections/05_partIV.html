Part IV

Fitting one plane

A linear problem inside a three-dimensional non-linear one. Why it is split that way, how the starting point is found without the reference, and what the reported errors mean.

19 · The two-level structure

Twenty-one numbers are unknown per plane: three geometric and eighteen charges. Searching all twenty-one would be hopeless. But the model is linear in the eighteen charges, so for any trial (p₀, w, t₀) the best charges can be solved exactly and instantly. That reduces the search to three dimensions.

19.1 Why non-negative least squares

Charge cannot be negative. Enforcing that is not cosmetic — an unconstrained solve will happily put −400 units in one bin and +900 in the next to chase noise, and those cancelling excursions leave the χ² almost unchanged while making the profile meaningless. NNLS forbids it.

The price is that the solution becomes sparse: bins that want to go negative are clipped to exactly zero. This surprises people, so it is worth showing why it happens.

Trap The median-truncation in the middle panel is not academic. It is exactly the bias that made the drift column read 24.7 mm and launched a hunt for a "missing 4 mm of gap". Taking means, trimmed means or rebinned medians instead moved the answer to 27.9 mm and the hardware question evaporated. Anything that summarises these profiles must account for their sparsity.

21 · Finding the starting point without the reference

This is the part of the fit that exists purely because production has no reference track. The R&D fits were seeded at the M3 position and angle, which is fine for a study and inadmissible in production — seeding from the reference and then measuring alignment or efficiency against the reference would be circular.

Seeding instead from the brightest strip and a local search lands in the wrong basin for about 17 % of planes, and those failures sit at 5° error with a higher χ² — they are genuinely missed minima, not disagreements. The production hit ladder is no help either: it is compressed by 15 %, so an inclined track's seed starts outside the basin.

So the code scans. _global_start() runs two nested grids:

wft/reco.py · _global_start 
stage 1  scan (p₀, t₀) at zero slope     7 × 11 = 77 evaluations
         p₀ : charge centroid ± 2.5 mm, step 0.5 mm
         t₀ : half-max seed ± 120 ns, step 40 ns
stage 2  scan (p₀, w) at the best t₀        11 × 21 = 231 evaluations
         w  : ±0.021 mm/ns  →  |tan θ| up to 0.57
# ~310 NNLS-profiled evaluations, ~0.15 s

21.1 A real limitation of the scan window

Notice something in the middle panel of Figure 24: the final Nelder-Mead result sits below the bottom edge of the scan box. That is not a drawing error, and it is worth understanding.

The p₀ scan is centred on the window's charge-weighted centroid. But p₀ is the track's position at the mesh, and on an inclined track those two differ by roughly half the transverse span — which for |tan θ| = 0.25 over a 29 mm gap is about 3.6 mm, more than the scan's ±2.5 mm half-width.

Open This looks like real headroom. Centring the p₀ scan on the earliest charge rather than the centroid, or widening P0_SCAN_HALF with |w| during stage 2, would put the true value inside the box for nearly every plane. The cost is a larger grid in stage 2 only. It has not been tried, and the numbers above are the case for trying it — the affected planes are 21 % of the sample and carry 5× the catastrophic-failure rate.

21.2 Coarse grid, then Nelder-Mead

From the scan's answer, fit_plane_raw() does one more coarse sweep — 7 t₀ values × 3 p₀ offsets × 5 w offsets = 105 evaluations — and then hands the best point to Nelder-Mead with an explicit initial simplex sized to the problem (0.4 mm in p₀, 1.5×10⁻³ mm/ns in w, 20 ns in t₀). The tolerances are loose on purpose: xatol = 10⁻³, fatol = 0.3, at most 140 iterations. Tightening them does not change the answer, because the per-event resolution is set by physics (§42), not by convergence.

Trap The explicit initial_simplex matters. Nelder-Mead's default simplex is a fixed relative perturbation of each coordinate, which for w ≈ 0.008 mm/ns is a step of 4×10⁻⁴ — far too small to escape the local structure — and for t₀ ≈ 400 ns is 20 ns, which is fine. Mixing units in a simplex method without setting the scales is a classic silent failure.

22 · The joint two-plane fit

A muon fires both planes at the same instant, so t0x and t0y are the same physical time up to a measured electronics offset. fit_joint() exploits that: one shared charge profile with a per-plane scale factor α, and a single t₀ with the Y plane offset by dt_xy.

At medium angles this changes nothing — t₀ was not the limiting parameter. Its value is for near-vertical planes, where the charge sits on one or two strips and timing carries almost no slope information. There the free single-plane slope can wander, and the other plane's timing pins it.

Measured Angle σ against |tan θ| for det3 single-plane fits: 1.03–1.10° everywhere above |tan θ| = 0.04, degrading to 1.42° (X) and 2.35° (Y) below it. Position is better at near-vertical, not worse, because the charge is concentrated. Hence the split advice: below TAN_MIN_SLOPE = 0.08, report the position and flag the slope.

23 · Errors, and what they include

The reported error on each parameter is the χ² curvature, scaled by √(χ²/dof) so that model imperfection inflates the error instead of being ignored, then added in quadrature with a measured physics floor.

FLOOR_TAN = 0.018
≈ 1.0°, the per-event angle resolution the detector physically has, measured by toy closure (§42). No fit can do better; quoting a smaller error would be a lie.
FLOOR_P0_MM = 0.33
the transverse charge-centroid jitter per 60 ns bin, from the same study.
Measured Do the errors mean anything? The pull — (fitted − reference) / reported error — has width 1.19 on X and 1.13 on Y over the full run (Figure 38). Slightly optimistic, but within 20 % of honest, which for a detector-physics error bar is a good place to be.

The three q_* quantities are deliberately raw quantiles rather than a fitted column length, because — per the trap in §19.1 — the right estimator depends on what you are measuring. Part VIII §50 builds the cathode map out of them.
