Part II

Why a per-strip time is not a drift time

The measurement that retired the old chain, re-done here from scratch. It is not an argument about estimators; it is a property of what the strips carry.

5 · Resistive strips share charge, late

The strips are not independent electrodes. A resistive layer sits over them, and the charge deposited on it does not stay put — it spreads sideways and bleeds onto the neighbouring strips over hundreds of nanoseconds. Each strip's waveform is therefore its own charge plus a delayed, dispersed copy of each neighbour's.

On det3 the ±1 neighbours each receive about 31 % of a strip's charge and the ±2 neighbours about 12 %. That is not a small correction. It is the reason the reconstruction in Part III exists.

6 · What that does to a hit time

Now put the two facts together. On an inclined track, neighbouring strips carry charge that arrived at different times, and each strip receives delayed copies from both sides. So:

  • A strip near the mesh — its own charge arrives early — also receives late copies from the strips further up the gap. Its single reported time is pulled later.
  • A strip near the cathode — its own charge arrives late — receives early copies from the strips nearer the mesh. Its time is pulled earlier.

Both ends squeeze inward. The time ladder is compressed. Read with the correct drift velocity, a compressed ladder describes a track that is too steep.

7 · The compression, measured

The claim is testable directly. For every strip of every reference-matched event, compute the strip's hit time and compare it with the drift time the M3 reference line implies for that strip's position, with the event's t₀ floated away. If the strips reported their own charge honestly, the residual would be flat and centred on zero.

Every estimator compresses the ladder by 13–15 % and therefore reports a drift velocity 15 % too high. The published production numbers were worse still (47–50 µm/ns) because the production hit time is a threshold crossing on a zero-suppressed waveform.

7.1 Why no better estimator helps

This is the part that took the longest to accept, so it is worth stating plainly. The four estimators in Figure 9 span the whole space of things people try: an early threshold, a fractional threshold, the peak, and the statistically optimal linear filter for the known pulse shape. They differ from each other by a few nanoseconds. They differ from the truth by the same S-curve.

They must, because they are all reading the same mixed waveform. A matched filter is optimal for extracting the arrival time of a pulse of the assumed shape; when the waveform is a sum of that pulse and two dispersed copies of different pulses at different times, the filter returns a weighted mean of the arrival times present, which is precisely the biased quantity. The mixing has to be modelled forward or removed, not thresholded away.

8 · What it costs

8.1 The signature that catches it

There is one diagnostic worth more than all the others, and it should be run on any reconstruction of this detector, including future ones. Take the fitted transverse speed w, divide by the reference track's tan θ, and you get an implied drift velocity for each event. The drift velocity is a property of the gas and the field. It cannot depend on how steep the muon was.

Rule Any change to the reconstruction, the calibration or the gas assumptions must be checked against implied-velocity flatness before its angles are believed. It is cheap, it needs no new data, and it has caught every calibration pathology in this analysis so far. mx_june_wft/03_angles.py computes it and writes the spread into angular_resolution.json.

9 · Forward model, not deconvolution

Given a known mixing kernel, the obvious move is to invert it — "unshare" the waveforms and recover each strip's own charge, then time that. This was tried and it works, in ensemble. Per event it does not, and the reason is structural: deconvolution amplifies noise at exactly the frequencies where the kernel is small, and a 32-sample window of a 17-strip cluster does not contain enough information to regularise that stably event by event.

The forward direction has no such problem. Instead of asking "what charge distribution, un-mixed, produced these waveforms?", it asks "does this track, mixed forward through the known kernel, look like these waveforms?" — and answers by generating the prediction and comparing. Generating is always well-conditioned. Searching over three geometric parameters is cheap. The neighbours' copies stop being contamination the moment they appear on both sides of the comparison.
