Why the pair vertex does not image the capsule, and the single tracks do

The two results are not in conflict. One is an ensemble centroid over millions of tracks; the other is a per-event position built from two of them, with a gain. ntof_athens_26/pair_vertex_imaging

The impression is right, and the two results do not contradict each other — they are different measurements. The single-track image is an ensemble centroid: the zero crossing of median(tan) against position, averaged over millions of tracks, whose error falls as 1/√N and reaches a few tenths of a millimetre. The pair vertex is a per-event position, so it inherits the full per-track resolution with nothing averaging it down. On this campaign that resolution is 44 mm in chamber A, 75 mm in C and 114 mm in D. There is no way to build a 10 mm image out of that, one pair at a time.

Three things then make it worse, and all three are measured below rather than argued.

1. The pairing amplifies the legs’ error instead of averaging it. Two lines in the transverse plane, each a known distance from the beam axis, meet at a point whose distance from the axis is fixed algebraically: |c| = √(e₁²+e₂²−2e₁e₂cosψ) / |sinψ|. That identity holds in the data to 1e-16, so the transverse pair vertex is a deterministic function of the two single-track pointings and carries no information they did not already have — and 1/|sinψ| is never below 1. Median amplification 1.12 for perpendicular pairs, 2.26 for intra and 2.86 for opposing, which is exactly the order the three classes come out in.

2. The 3D closest approach spends transverse accuracy on a coordinate the source does not localise. The capsule is 10 mm across but 80 mm long along the beam, so there is no y image to find; and the y view is not a pointing measurement in any case — its band slope is 0.26 (A), 0.35 (C), 0.06 (D) where a point source gives 1. The two legs consequently disagree in y by a median of 158 mm, and the 3D fit slides both tracks along themselves to reconcile that, dragging the vertex off the transverse answer. Dropping y outright halves it: perpendicular 46 → 26 mm, opposing 112 → 51 mm.

3. Nothing here is a geometry problem or a bug. Replace both legs’ directions with directions that point exactly at a random point in the capsule, keeping their measured impact points, and every class returns 7.1 mm with 100.0% inside the bore — 7.1 mm being the median radius of a uniform 10 mm disc, i.e. the capsule itself. The crossing geometry imposes no floor of its own; the whole of the loss is the per-track angle.

What this means practically. The pair vertex is not broken and it is not a second measurement of the target — it is the two legs’ own pointing, combined with a gain of 1–3. So it should be read as a track-quality variable, not as imaging, and the imaging claim should continue to rest on the band crossing. If a per-event vertex is wanted, it exists already: at a 5 mm per-leg cut the transverse crossing puts 96.6% of perpendicular pairs inside the capsule, against 8.0% at the published 30 mm cut — on 946 pairs of 29,617. That is a real capsule image from pairs; it costs 96.8% of the sample and it is, by the algebra above, a restatement of the leg cut.

Sample: 62,729 real pairs and as many event-mixed, over 33 runs of the condor full pass, built at a 60 mm leg ceiling so the published 30 mm is one point on a scan. At that 30 mm point the sample is pair-for-pair identical to the published pair_qa table on all ten arm pairs — median leg miss 8.5 and 20.8 mm, median vertex 46–112 mm.

The event-mixed null does not mean here what it means in the opening-angle spectrum. Mixing decorrelates the trigger, not the origin: two tracks from two different neutron captures in the same capsule both still came out of that capsule, so a mixed pair has a genuine common source and a working vertex detector would image it just as well. Real ≈ mixed in the figures below is therefore not evidence that the imaging failed. It is the separate (and expected) statement that the vertex carries no information about whether the two legs shared a trigger.

The null shuffles tan among the tracks of one chamber. It keeps the impact points, the angular distribution and the acceptance, and destroys only the correlation between where a track landed and which way it was going — which is the whole of “this track came from the capsule”. So the difference between the two columns is the pointing information, in units anyone can check.

There is real pointing information: chamber A’s median miss is half its null and its 10 mm fraction is 2.88× it. But the last column is the one to keep: even inside 10 mm, between a third and a half of the tracks are accounted for by a sample with no source in it at all. Chamber D barely beats its own null (114.1 against 119.3 mm), and a quarter of its x plane is dead, which is the structure visible in its panel.

5. The angle scale: it moves the vertex, and it is not the explanation

The band crossing is −intercept/slope, so multiplying every angle by s scales the numerator and the denominator together and the crossing does not move. Over the whole scan it moves by 0.0 mm — that is not an approximation, it is the exact cancellation being displayed. The vertex uses the angle’s magnitude and has no such protection: at s = 1.33, the factor the arm-A scintillator wall independently asks for, the perpendicular vertex goes from 25.8 to 54.9 mm.

So the angle scale matters to the vertex and not to the image, which is half the answer to the original question. It is not, however, the cause of the failure: the scan has a minimum, and at that minimum the vertex is still 25.4 mm rather than 7.

Is what is left after the cut resolution, or background?

The leg scan says a tighter pointing cut sharpens the vertex. That is not the same statement as “the sample is dirty”: a pointing cut sharpens the vertex on a perfectly pure sample too, because it keeps the tracks whose angle happened to come out well. The question that separates the two is whether, at a fixed pointing cut, a scintillator-confirmed leg beats an unconfirmed one. det_a_scint supplies that for arm A.

Intra pairs show a small but consistent lift of 1.12–1.28, in the same direction as the 2.06 ± 0.29 that det_a_intra found on its slope-selected sample. Opposing pairs sit below one — a real A–C pair is less likely to have a tight vertex than a mixed one — which is the trigger talking, not the physics: the production trigger is single-arm, so an A-triggered event is already depleted in C tracks and the surviving ones are a different population. That is the same effect pairs.py was built to control for.

A lift of 1 is not “no signal” and it is not “the imaging failed”. Both legs of a mixed pair still came out of the same capsule, so a perfect vertex detector would put mixed pairs on the capsule too. What this table says is narrower: the vertex cannot be used as a pair selection, and a cut on it removes signal and background alike.

What this does not rule out

  • It does not say the tracking is wrong. Every test here is consistent with a reconstruction whose per-track angular resolution is what it is. The geometry checks out (the closed form to 1e-16, the ideal-leg substitution returning the source exactly), and nothing points at a sign, a frame or a pairing error.
  • It does not measure the angular resolution itself. The miss distance at the axis folds together the angle error, the real source size, any scattering in the chamber walls and the non-target background. Separating those needs a sample with an independent direction reference, which is what det_a_scint’s wall-and-plastic lever arm is for and what a per-detector recalibration would produce.
  • It does not settle the angle scale. Section 5 records that the gated sample's focus prefers s ≤ 1 while the wall prefers 1.33, on two different samples, and leaves it open.
  • It says nothing about the opening angle. The opening angle is a difference of two directions and does not use the vertex at all, so none of this touches the campaign spectra. It does mean that a vertex cut cannot be used to clean them.
  • Chamber B is in the tables for completeness only. It has no field-shaping rings, no uniform drift field and no usable angle; its rows are printed so that they can be seen to be empty of information, not because they are a measurement.
