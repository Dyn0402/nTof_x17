\intent{The result specific to this readout: both views see the same charge, and that is what tells which X segment
belongs to which Y segment when two particles cross one chamber.}

A chamber with two strip views has no direct way to determine which segment in the X view belongs to which segment in
the Y view. With one particle in the chamber there is only one segment per view, and no ambiguity. With two particles
there are two ways to pair the segments, and the wrong pairing produces two ghost tracks. The two views do not share a
coordinate. In this design, however, they share the charge: every avalanche lands on pads that alternate between the X
and the Y strips, so both views read the same charge at the same time.

\subsection{The two views see the same charge}\label{sec:balance}
\Crefp{fig:pairing}{a} shows this charge balance for single tracks. We define $f=q_X/(q_X+q_Y)$ as the fraction of the
charge read by the X strips. Its median is 0.485 in chamber~A and 0.513 in chamber~B, and between 0.485 and 0.509 in
the other chambers. Track by track, the ratio $q_X/q_Y$ of chamber~A scatters by 6\,\% (a core spread of 0.014 in $f$;
0.017 to 0.028 in the other chambers). The balance does not depend on the position or the angle of the track.
Chamber~D has the same core, but a quarter of its tracks lie in a tail, which is caused by the fault of its X
readout.

\subsection{Finding the muon when it is not alone}\label{sec:twoeff}
In 3.2\,\% of the events (931 of 29\,398), a second particle crosses chamber~A together with the muon. We identify
these events by a second segment that appears in both views with matching charge. They occur in three configurations
(\cref{fig:twoparticle}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item The two particles are separate in both views (662 events). In this case the muon is detected in
  $95.6\pm0.8$\,\% of the events, as often as when it is alone.
  \item The two particles are separate in one view and merged in the projection of the other (176 events). The muon is
  detected in $47\pm4$\,\% of these events.
  \item The muon is clean in both views and a separate, large deposit lies elsewhere in the plane (93 events). The
  event then exceeds the 50 strips above which the reconstruction treats an event as a discharge and fits nothing. The
  muon is detected in 1\,\% of these events.
\end{itemize}
Over all three configurations, the muon is detected in $77.0\pm1.4$\,\% of the events. The reconstruction addresses
the second configuration with a joint fit. This fit offers a second track to a merged column when the charge of the
other view supports it. On a fixed set of 195 merged events, the joint fit raises the detection of the muon from 41 to
54\,\%, and it does not split a clean single muon in any of the five chambers. The pairs that remain unsplit either
share almost no depth or lie within four strip pitches of each other.

\subsection{Pairing the views}\label{sec:pairing}
To test the pairing we use 567 two-particle events in which the reference track identifies one of the two particles
in both views, so that the correct pairing is known. \Crefp{fig:pairing}{b} shows the charge ratio of the correct
pairing and of the swapped pairing in these events. The correct pairing lies on the single-track balance, while the
swapped pairing is spread over a factor of several. If we choose the pairing whose charge ratios are closer to the
single-track value, the choice is correct in 95.6\,\% of the events.

The drift time provides a second criterion. The charge of one particle arrives at the same times in both views, while
the charge of two particles at different depths does not. The median arrival times of a correct pair agree to 48\,ns,
and those of a swapped pair to 176\,ns (\crefp{fig:pairing}{d}). Using the charge and the time together, the pairing
is correct in
\[ 97.7\pm0.6\ \%.\]
The failures occur where they are expected (\crefp{fig:pairing}{c}). Every event in which the two particles differ in
charge by more than a factor 1.65 is paired correctly (290 of 290), and in all of the failures the two particles have
nearly equal charge. The reconstruction makes the same choice with a cost function that includes further timing terms.
On the same 567 events it pairs $96.6\pm0.8$\,\% correctly~\cite{paperII}.

\subsection{How close two tracks can be}\label{sec:separation}
Real two-particle events cannot tell us how close two tracks can be before they are no longer resolved. The second
particle has no reference track, and the separation is unknown precisely in the events where the tracks merge. We
therefore make the measurement by overlay. The waveforms of two real single-muon events of chamber~A are added, each
at its own position, and the sum is passed through the same reconstruction. Each muon keeps its own reference track.
Both muons were reconstructed when alone, so the result is relative to the single-track efficiency. As a check, an
overlay with an empty partner event returns the single-track result (200 of 200 found, with the single-track
resolution).

\Crefp{fig:separation}{a} shows the probability that both muons are reconstructed, as a function of the separation of
the two tracks in the view where they are closer. Here, both muons count as reconstructed if each is found within
5\,mm of its own reference and has its two views paired correctly. Above 18\,mm this probability is 94 to 96\,\%.
Between 3 and 18\,mm it is lower, falling from $74\pm3$\,\% to $53\pm3$\,\% as the separation decreases. Below 3\,mm,
which is about four strip pitches, it is $11\pm2$\,\%. Without the requirement on the pairing, these probabilities are
2 to 7 points higher. The limit is therefore resolving the two tracks, and not pairing them.

When both muons are reconstructed, the opening angle of the pair is measured with a resolution of 1.2 to
1.5\textdegree{} in space at every separation (\crefp{fig:separation}{b}). This is close to $\sqrt2$ times the
resolution of one track. The separation of the two tracks is measured to 0.8 to 1.1\,mm above 3\,mm
(\crefp{fig:separation}{c}). A chamber therefore measures the opening angle of a pair that it contains about as well
as two independent tracks allow, down to a separation of 18\,mm. Below this separation it still measures the opening
angle, but with decreasing efficiency.

Three caveats apply. First, both particles are minimum-ionizing muons within $\pm20$\textdegree{} of the normal.
Second, the electronic noise is added twice under the second track. Third, the summed signal saturates at the limit of
the ADC. In addition, the two tracks start at the same time, which is correct for a pair from one decay but not for an
accidental coincidence.
