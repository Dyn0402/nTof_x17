\intent{The chamber calibrates itself. The drift velocity is measured two independent ways and the gap a third; they
agree; one physics model with one free number, the water content, describes all of it; and the same analysis sees
oxygen where there is some.}

A TPC measures the depth at which charge was deposited in its active volume from the time the charge takes to drift to the mesh, and the drift velocity $\vd$ converts one into the other. The angle of a track is reconstructed as $\tan\theta=w/\vd$, where
$w$ is the transverse drift rate of \cref{sec:reco}: the distance the arriving charge moves across the strips per unit
of drift time. All of the tracking results of \cref{sec:tracking} therefore depend on $\vd$. The drift velocity depends
strongly on the gas: Ar/iC$_4$H$_{10}$ 95/5\,\% was used on the cosmic bench but no contamination measurements were made.
For this reason we measure the drift velocity from the data themselves. We do this with two independent methods, and
comparing the two also gives a measurement of the drift gap.

\subsection{Two velocities and the gap}\label{sec:gap}
The first method for measuring the drift velocity uses only the timing of the signal. A track near normal incidence deposits charge uniformly across the drift gap. If the signals of all strips are summed, the resulting signal begins when the electrons produced next
to the mesh arrive and ends when the electrons produced at the drift cathode arrive. We call this summed signal the
\emph{drift column} (\crefp{fig:twov}{a}). Its duration is $T=g/\vd$, where $g$ is the drift gap. We define the
\emph{timing velocity} as $g/T$. This method requires the gap to be known.

The second method uses the reference tracks and does not require the gap. For inclined tracks, the fitted transverse
rate $w$ is proportional to the slope of the reference track, and the constant of proportionality is the drift
velocity: $w=w_0+\vd\tan\theta_\mathrm{ref}$, where $\theta_\mathrm{ref}$ is the angle of the reference track and $w_0$
is a small offset caused by the bow of the readout board (\cref{sec:bow}; \crefp{fig:twov}{b}). We call this the \emph{angle-scale velocity}, since it is the velocity for which the
fitted angles agree with those of the reference tracks.

The gap itself can be measured from geometry, with no drift velocity involved. An inclined track crosses the gap over
a length $L$ along the strips, so that $g=L/\tan\theta$. This gives $28.19\pm0.19$\,mm for chamber~A. The product of
the angle-scale velocity and the duration of the drift column is a second, independent measurement of the same gap.
Over four drift fields between 248 and 390\,V/cm, three from chamber~A's drift scan and the operating point, this
product gives $28.4\pm0.3$\,mm ($\chi^2=2.9$ for 3 degrees of freedom; \crefp{fig:twov}{c}). At the operating point
we sum Run~1 and Run~2. Run~2 was taken the following night, in drier gas. Its drift time is 2\,\% shorter and its
angle-scale velocity 1.4\,\% higher than those of Run~1, so the two runs give the same gap ($28.6\pm0.3$\,mm and
$28.4\pm0.2$\,mm). The two velocity measurements and the geometric gap are therefore consistent
to 2\,\%. At the lowest field, 177\,V/cm, the drift column lasts about 1800\,ns, which is longer than the recorded
waveform (\crefp{fig:twov}{a}). The measured duration at this field is therefore cut by the end of the record, and the
gap obtained from it is only a lower limit.

The same product of the angle-scale velocity and the duration of the drift column gives the gap of the other four
chambers. It is 31.1\,mm for chamber~B, 28.7\,mm for chamber~C, 29.7\,mm for chamber~D and 29.5\,mm for chamber~E,
each with an uncertainty of 0.3 to 0.4\,mm. The geometric gaps of these chambers agree with these values within 1\,mm.
The gaps of the five chambers therefore range from 28.4\,mm (chamber~A) to 31.1\,mm (chamber~B). Their mean is
29.5\,mm, and they scatter by 1.0\,mm rms around the design value of 30\,mm. The design value is set by the aluminum
frame that acts as the spacer, while the cathode foil is mounted on a drift window that is assembled by hand. We
therefore attribute the spread between chambers to the construction of the drift windows. We have no direct
measurement of the position of the cathode in any chamber.

The gap is not the same everywhere on chamber~A. To measure how it varies, we divide the interior of the chamber (at
least 30\,mm from the edge of the active area) into cells of about 100\,mm and measure the gap in each cell. We use the
two long runs at the operating point, Run~1 and Run~2, and analyze them separately. In each cell we make three
independent measurements. The first is the geometric gap $L/\tan\theta$ with the X strips, as above. The second is the
same geometric measurement with the Y strips, which are read by other electronics. The third is the product of the
angle-scale velocity and the duration of the drift column, both measured in the cell. For each of the three
measurements, Run~1 and Run~2 agree within their uncertainties. The three measurements also agree with each other.
Across the cells, their values are correlated with coefficients between 0.8 and 0.95.

All three measurements show the same pattern. The gap increases along $x$ by 0.4 to 0.8\,mm per 100\,mm. This
corresponds to a tilt of the cathode relative to the mesh of about 0.2 to 0.5\textdegree. Along $y$, the gap is
largest near the center and shorter toward both ends, by about 0.5\,mm at 100\,mm from the center. Over the interior
the gap therefore ranges from about 27 to about 29\,mm, and the cell values scatter by 0.5 to 0.7\,mm around the mean
of 28.2\,mm. The product of the velocity and the duration shows the same pattern as the geometric gap, but with about
0.7 of its amplitude. The size of the variation is therefore known to about 30\,\%.

This variation is a change of the gap, and not a change of the gas. At a fixed drift voltage, a shorter gap means a
higher drift field. Since the drift velocity increases with the field, a shorter gap shortens the drift time by more
than the change of the gap itself. Magboltz predicts $\mathrm{d}\ln\vd/\mathrm{d}\ln T=-0.41$ for a change of the gap,
while a change of the gas composition would give $-1$. We measure this ratio on the part of the variation that is
finer than a smooth surface, and we take the velocity from one run and the drift time from the other run, so that the
noise of a cell does not enter. The result is $-0.41\pm0.08$. The smooth part of the variation gives $-0.45\pm0.03$
with the velocity of the Y strips. With the velocity of the X strips it gives $-0.74\pm0.02$, which is neither value.
The velocity of the X strips therefore varies across the chamber by more than the gap can explain, and we have not
identified the reason.

The drift time alone gives a map of the gap, without the reference tracks, since the drift time of a track changes
with the local gap. With this map we find that the pattern above belongs to one mounting of the chamber and not to the
chamber itself. We have data from two mountings of chamber~A in the test stand, on 25~June and on 27 and
28~June. Run~1 and Run~2 were taken in the second one. The two mountings give two different maps of the gap
(\cref{app:gapmap}, \cref{fig:gapmount}). Within one mounting the map does not change with time, with the mesh voltage
or with the drift voltage between 700 and 1100\,V. The dish of the readout board, in contrast, is the same in the two
mountings. The cathode is a foil of 50\,\textmu m on a removable drift window. Either the foil took a different shape
when the chamber was moved, or a different drift window was mounted: the windows could be exchanged between chambers
between runs, and we have no record of which window was on which chamber. The mean gap, however, does not change. On 25~June the drift velocity is
22\,\textmu m/ns, against 37\,\textmu m/ns in Run~1 and Run~2, and the product of the velocity and the drift time
still gives 28.2\,mm.

\Cref{app:gapmap} describes these measurements. It shows that the drift lines are tilted and bent by the shapes of
the mesh and of the cathode, as measured directly from the signals, and that the tilt can be predicted from the dish
of the board and the map of the gap. A map of the drift direction made for one mounting improves the angle resolution
of chamber~A in that mounting by 26\,\%.

\subsection{Drift velocity against field: one curve, one number}\label{sec:vE}
\Cref{fig:vE} shows both measured drift velocities of chamber~A as a function of the drift field. The timing velocity increases from
25.2 to 37.2\,\textmu m/ns between fields of 248 and 390\,V/cm, and the angle-scale velocity from 2.55 to 38.9\,\textmu m/ns over
seven drift fields. These velocities are not consistent with dry Ar/iC$_4$H$_{10}$, for which the drift velocity would
be about 40\,\textmu m/ns at the fields used here. They are, however, well described by a
Magboltz~\cite{Biagi1999} calculation in which the water content of the gas is the only free parameter. A fit to the
timing velocity gives a water content of 0.96\,\% (68\,\% interval 0.94--0.98\,\%; $\chi^2=2.6$ for 3 degrees of
freedom). The angle-scale velocities were not used in the fit. Above 100\,V/cm, they lie within $-2.6$ to $+2.1\,\%$ of
the fitted curve. At 177\,V/cm the drift column is longer than the recorded waveform (\cref{sec:gap}). The timing velocity of
17.7\,\textmu m/ns at this field is therefore only an upper limit, and it is not used in the fit.

The other chambers have no drift-field scan. For them we have only the drift velocity of their long runs, each at a
single field. A single velocity can always be matched by some water content, so it does not test the model. It can
only be expressed as an equivalent water content: 0.69\,\% for B, 1.00\,\% for C, 0.48\,\% for D and 0.42\,\% for E.
This equivalent water content differs between chambers by more than a factor of two, which is one reason we use the
drift velocity measured in each run (\cref{sec:drying}). Water at this level has a large effect: a water content of
one percent lowers the drift velocity at 250\,V/cm by about 40\,\%.

\subsection{The gas dries over hours}\label{sec:drying}
The water content also changes with time. Over the night of 26--27 June, the drift velocity of chambers C and D rose
steadily (\crefp{fig:drying}{a}). To interpret this rise, we invert the Magboltz curve at each chamber's drift field
to convert the measured velocity into a water content. The water content falls from 1.47 to 0.76\,\% in chamber~C and
from 0.68 to 0.35\,\% in chamber~D (\crefp{fig:drying}{b}). The excess of water over the final plateau follows the same
shape in time in both chambers (\crefp{fig:drying}{c}; correlation 0.975). The two chambers reach different plateaus,
however, so the gas in the two chambers is not the same. Chamber~A dried in the same way but more slowly: its drift
velocity rose by 1.8\,\% between Run~1 and the end of Run~2, from 35.8 to 36.6\,\textmu m/ns (\cref{app:gain}).

This has a practical consequence for a TPC of this kind. The water content is at the percent level and can change by
a factor of two overnight. If the drift velocity were taken from the nominal gas mixture, or from an earlier run,
every reconstructed angle would carry a scale error of tens of percent. For this reason the reconstruction takes
$\vd$ from the data of each run. In effect, the measured drift velocity serves as the gas monitor of the bench.

\subsection{Oxygen: charge lost along the drift}\label{sec:attachment}
Water in the gas slows the drift. Oxygen has a different effect: drifting electrons attach to it and are lost before
they reach the mesh. This loss can be seen in the drift column. A track near normal incidence deposits the same charge
at every depth, so the drift column (the summed signal as a function of time) shows how much of the charge from each
depth survives the drift. On the bench, the drift column of chamber~A is flat until its end
(\crefp{fig:attach_charge}{a}), which indicates that no charge is lost. In the beam, chamber~E ran with two other
mixtures, Ar/CF$_4$/iC$_4$H$_{10}$ and Ar/CO$_2$/iC$_4$H$_{10}$, and in both of them its drift column falls with drift time. To
describe this we use the same Magboltz model, now including drift, diffusion and attachment, on a grid of water and
oxygen contents. The model fits the two drift columns with 226 and 403\,ppm of oxygen. With the same water content and
no oxygen, the model column stays flat and does not describe the data (\crefp{fig:attach_charge}{b, c}). The method
therefore finds oxygen in the runs where charge is lost and none in the run where it is not. The water and oxygen
contents during the beam test are shown as a function of time in \cref{app:beam}.
