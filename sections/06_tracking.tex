\subsection{Angular resolution}\label{sec:angle}
We measure the angular resolution with cosmic muons, by comparing the angle fitted in the chamber to the angle of
the reference track. Only tracks in the interior of the chamber are used (\cref{sec:definitions}). \Cref{fig:angle}
shows the result for chamber~A. Runs 1 and 2 together contain 25\,400 matched single tracks per view, and for these
tracks the resolution is
\[ s_{68}=0.76^\circ\ \text{(X)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0.69^\circ\ \text{(Y)}, \]
with a median residual below 0.02\textdegree.

\emph{How the angle is obtained.} The reconstruction fits the transverse rate of the charge across the strips, and the
angle follows from this rate and the drift velocity (\cref{sec:reco}). This conversion assumes that the drift lines are
straight and perpendicular to the readout plane. In the chamber they are not, because the mesh and the cathode are
not flat (\cref{sec:bow,app:gapmap}). We therefore convert the rate into an angle with a map of the drift direction.
The map is a smooth function of the position of the track in the chamber, which gives the tilt of the drift lines and
a local scale of the angle. We fit it to the reference tracks. We make one map for each mounting of the chamber,
because the cathode changes its shape when the chamber is mounted again, and a map made in one mounting does not
describe another (\cref{app:gapmap}). We use the map only in the interior of the chamber, which is the region that
the reference tracks cover (30\,mm or more from the border). Closer to the border, the angle keeps the earlier
conversion, the quadratic bow block of \cref{sec:bow}. The map is used for 91\,\% of the tracks of
chamber~A.

Because the map is fitted to the reference tracks, a resolution measured on the tracks of the fit would be too good.
All resolutions of this section are therefore cross-validated. We divide the data of a mounting into five blocks of
consecutive time. The angle of a track is computed with a map, and with the constants of the conversion, that were
fitted on the four other blocks. The map has no information on the track that it is applied to.

Four properties of this result are important, and we discuss them in turn.

\emph{The resolution is that of the chamber.} The angle of the reference track is known far better than the angle
measured by the chamber. The reference telescope and the multiple scattering of the muons between its planes
contribute about 0.05\textdegree{} to the residual. The data bound that contribution at 0.11\textdegree{} in X and
0.14\textdegree{} in Y. Added in quadrature to the resolution of chamber~A, the contribution of 0.05\textdegree{} changes the result by less
than 0.5\,\%, and the bound of 0.11\textdegree{} (X) and 0.14\textdegree{} (Y) by at most 1\,\% and 2\,\%.

the bow block alone, the two runs gave $0.98^\circ$ and $0.90^\circ$ (Run~1) and $0.99^\circ$ and $0.90^\circ$
(Run~2).

\emph{The resolution depends little on the track angle.} The bench provides track angles within $\pm20$\textdegree.
Between $-16$ and $+16$\textdegree{} the resolution stays between 0.65 and 0.85\textdegree{}. It rises to 0.9--1.1\textdegree{}
in the outer 4\textdegree{} of the range, where each bin holds only 50 to 340 tracks (\crefp{fig:angle}{c}). The bow block alone showed the same rise, from 0.9 to 1.0--1.2\textdegree. In the beam,
where chamber~E was yawed by 25.4\textdegree{}, the angle scale is $0.98\pm0.03$ of the known angle~\cite{paperII}.
The angle scale therefore remains correct at an angle larger than those the bench can reach.

\emph{The resolution includes a correction of the drift direction.} The readout boards are bowed, and the cathode is
not flat. This tilts the drift direction by an amount that varies across the chamber (\cref{sec:bow,app:gapmap}). The
map corrects for this tilt. With the quadratic bow block alone, the resolution of chamber~A on the same tracks is
0.99\textdegree{} and 0.90\textdegree, and with no correction at all it is 1.10\textdegree{} and 1.07\textdegree.
The map therefore improves the resolution by 23\,\% in X and 23\,\% in Y relative to the bow block.

The distribution of the residual has a Gaussian core and a tail (\crefp{fig:angle}{b}). A Gaussian fitted within
$\pm2\,s_{68}$ of the median has a width of $\sigma=0.70^\circ$ (X) and $0.63^\circ$ (Y), and this range contains 93
and 92\,\% of the tracks. With 25\,000 tracks, the core is close to a Gaussian but not exactly one
($\chi^2/\mathrm{ndf}=26/13$ and $73/11$). Because of the tail, $s_{68}$ is 9--10\,\% larger than $\sigma$. The map
narrows the core, but it does not change the tail. The tail is roughly flat: in each view, 3.4\,\% of the tracks have a
residual larger than 5\textdegree, with the map as with the bow block alone. For these tracks the
fitted angle is far steeper than the true one, so they are not simply the edge of a wider core. They are the events
discussed in \cref{sec:straight}, in which a $\delta$-ray or a second charge deposit pulls the fit of a single
straight track away from the muon.

Chamber~B, the second good chamber, has a resolution of $0.80^\circ$ and $0.90^\circ$ (not shown), against $1.05^\circ$
and $1.13^\circ$ with the bow block alone. Chambers C, D and E have resolutions of $1.08/1.35^\circ$,
$1.03/1.15^\circ$ and $2.33/2.02^\circ$. They are the chambers with the defects described in \cref{sec:datasets}
(\cref{fig:effmap}). These resolutions are cross-validated in the same way, with conditions that differ
between the chambers. In chamber~C, a fifth of the tracks have a drift column that reaches the end of the depth grid
of the fit, and these tracks are reconstructed too flat by 0.3 to 1.2\textdegree. We rescale their angle with a factor
fitted on these tracks, and we keep them. In chambers D and E, the same tracks are 6--17\,\% (D) and 8--9\,\% (E) of the
sample, and we leave them out. The resolutions of C, D and E with the bow block alone, on all tracks, are
$1.26/1.51^\circ$, $1.23/1.46^\circ$ and $2.41/2.08^\circ$. For D, the X view has a map only for $x\ge200$\,mm.
For chamber~E there is no map. A joint map of its two days improves its resolution by 0.2\,\% in X and 4\,\% in Y,
and it makes the edge of the chamber worse, so the numbers of chamber~E are those of the bow block.

\emph{Where there is no map.} We have no map for the data of chamber~A of 22 and 23 June, and none for the first
day of chamber~B (17 June). On 22 June the gas of chamber~A was wet and its drift velocity was low. The drift time of
four out of five tracks is cut by the end of the depth grid of the fit, so there is no usable angle to fit a map on.
On 23 June the fitted angles of chamber~A are not correlated with the reference angle at all (correlation 0.01),
and no conversion can repair them. The first day of chamber~B has only 104 tracks that can be used. The two points
of the drift-field scan of chamber~A with the lowest fields (33 and 100\,V/cm) have no map either, because their
angle resolution is 9.1 and 2.0\textdegree{} already without one. The resolution of chamber~A quoted in this paper
does not use any of these data, except the point at 100\,V/cm of \cref{app:gain}, which is quoted without a map.

\paragraph{What it is compared with.} It is useful to compare this resolution with two other numbers. The first is
the resolution of a conventional micro-TPC analysis, which extracts one time per strip and fits a straight line to
these times. Applied to the same chamber~A events, and evaluated with the same statistic, the conventional analysis
gives 2.1\textdegree{} and 2.7\textdegree, while the forward-model fit with the bow block gives 1.0\textdegree~\cite{paperII}.
With the map of the drift direction the forward-model fit gives 0.7--0.8\textdegree. The second number is
the resolution expected for an ideal chamber. A simulation of ideal events, passed through the same reconstruction,
gives 0.3--0.5\textdegree. The measured resolution is therefore 1.5 to 2 times larger than what the geometry
and the sampling of the chamber allow. Part of this excess comes from events with saturated strips or with an
unusually large charge. The rest behaves like a fluctuation of the charge pattern from event to event that is larger
in the data than in the simulation. This fluctuation is characterized in the companion paper, but we have not
identified its origin in the signal.

\subsection{What the resolution means for a pair}\label{sec:pointing}
\Cref{sec:requirement} set the requirement of an angular resolution of about one degree. This scale is set by the
scattering of the leptons before they are measured. Chamber~A meets this requirement. To see what this means for a
pair, consider again the symmetric pair at the $^4$He edge, with two leptons of 9.8\,MeV. The resolution of the two
chambers alone would give an uncertainty on the opening angle of 1.3\textdegree. The scattering in 20\,cm of air
alone gives 2.0\textdegree, and the two effects together give 2.4\textdegree{} (\cref{sec:requirement}). The chamber
is therefore not what limits the measurement of the opening angle, and a chamber with a better resolution would
improve it only slightly. With the resolution of chamber~A, 0.7\textdegree{} instead of 1\textdegree, the two effects
together give 2.2\textdegree{} instead of 2.4\textdegree.

The same track can be extrapolated back to test whether the particle came from the target. Using
\cref{eq:pointing}, a track with an angular resolution of 0.76\textdegree{} (0.69\textdegree) points back to a
target 230\,mm away with an uncertainty of 3.0\,mm (2.8\,mm). The position resolution, discussed in the next
subsection, contributes only 0.2\,\% of that variance. For the leptons these chambers were built to measure, the
multiple scattering in the air and in the entrance foils of the chamber produces a larger uncertainty at the target
than this at every energy: 4.5\,mm at 16\,MeV, 8.8\,mm at 8\,MeV and 17\,mm at 4\,MeV (\cref{tab:material}). Here
again, the resolution of the chamber itself is not the limit. For the same reason, the material of the entrance foils
deserves attention. The copper of the cathode alone, if its thickness is the 9\,\textmu m assumed here, scatters the
leptons as much as the 20\,cm of air.

\subsection{Position resolution}\label{sec:position}
We measure the position resolution from the residual between the reconstructed position of the track at the mesh
and the reference ray. For chamber~A, this residual has a Gaussian core of 0.55\,mm in X and 0.53\,mm in Y. Only a
small part of this width comes from the chamber. The residual contains three contributions: the resolution of the
chamber, the pointing resolution of the reference ray (0.24 and 0.21\,mm), and the scattering of the muon between the
reference planes. The scattering can be recognized because it makes the residual grow with the $\chi^2$ of the
reference track. To remove it, we take tracks near normal incidence and extrapolate the core width to $\chi^2\to0$
(\crefp{fig:posres}{a}). This leaves $275\pm15$ and $236\pm18$\,\textmu m. We then subtract the pointing resolution
in quadrature, which leaves the contribution of the chamber: $128\pm33$\,\textmu m in X and $113\pm36$\,\textmu m in
Y. This subtraction dominates the uncertainty. If the pointing resolution is changed by 10\,\%, the results move to
63--166 and 62--145\,\textmu m (\crefp{fig:posres}{b}). We therefore quote an intrinsic resolution of about
$120\pm60$\,\textmu m for chamber~A near normal incidence. The same procedure for chamber~B gives $163\pm29$ and
$176\pm21$\,\textmu m.

The beam data provide an independent check with a different reference. In its working region (\cref{app:beam}),
chamber~E has a resolution of $138$\,\textmu m in X and $120$\,\textmu m in Y after the pointing resolution of the
telescope is subtracted. The uncertainty on the resolution of the telescope gives ranges of 112--153 and
89--136\,\textmu m. If nothing is assumed about the telescope, the upper bounds are 153 and 145\,\textmu m
(\crefp{fig:posres}{c--e}). Chamber~E operates at reduced gain over most of its board, so we treat this as a
cross-check rather than as the main result. The two setups agree.

\subsection{Time resolution}\label{sec:time}
For each view, the reconstruction returns the time at which the charge of a track begins to arrive. We call the
time of the track, measured relative to the scintillator trigger, the event time. For chamber~A the event time has a
resolution of 41.6\,ns in Run~1 and 40.3\,ns in Run~2. The resolution can be separated into two parts. The difference
between the times of the two views cancels everything the two views have in common, so the width of
$(t_X-t_Y)/\sqrt2$ gives the part of the resolution that is uncorrelated between the views. This part is 22.4 and
22.2\,ns. The part that is common to the two views is 38 and 37\,ns. In a TPC a time corresponds to a depth: at a
drift velocity of 36\,\textmu m/ns, the event time resolution corresponds to 1.5\,mm along the drift direction.
Chambers B and D have event time resolutions of 42 and 46\,ns, and chamber~C of 78\,ns (\cref{app:gain}).

The time resolution is determined by the size of the signal, as the next subsection shows. A correction for time walk
changes it by less than 1\,\%. Its dependence on the drift field is shown in \cref{app:gain}.

\subsection{What the gain buys: time, and nothing else}\label{sec:gain}
To study how the performance depends on the gain, the voltage $HV_\mathrm{resist}$ of chamber~A was scanned from
425 to 525\,V at a fixed drift field (\cref{fig:gainscan}). Between 425 and 490\,V, the charge of a track increases
by a factor of $13.1\pm0.5$. On average the charge doubles every $17.5\pm0.3$\,V (every 19\,V at the bottom of the
range and every 15\,V at the top). Over the same range, the fraction of tracks with at least one strip at the limit
of the ADC rises from zero to 30\,\%.

While the charge changes by this factor of 13, the angular resolution does not change. It stays within
0.68--0.81\textdegree{} in X and 0.62--0.74\textdegree{} in Y up to 505\,V, with an angle scale of 0.995--1.005 in both
views (\crefp{fig:gainscan}{b}). These resolutions use the map of the drift direction of the mounting, cross-validated
in blocks of time, as in \cref{sec:angle}. The core of the position residual does not change either
(\crefp{fig:gainscan}{c}), and neither does the efficiency (\cref{sec:hvscan}). The time resolution, however,
improves strongly with the gain. Its uncorrelated part falls from $83\pm10$\,ns at 425\,V to $24\pm1$\,ns at 490\,V,
and the event time resolution falls from $102\pm8$ to $38\pm2$\,ns (\crefp{fig:gainscan}{d}).

The reason for this difference is that the time and the angle are measured from different parts of the signal. The
time is taken from the start of the drift column, which is made by the first electrons to arrive, those produced
next to the mesh. This is a small part of the signal, and its timing degrades as its amplitude approaches the noise.
The angle, in contrast, is fitted to the whole drift column, using every strip and every sample. Two measurements
support this explanation. First, we look track by track at the unsaturated tracks of the scan points below 510\,V,
and find that the angle residual does not depend on the charge of the track over a factor 17 in charge. Second, we
select tracks in a fixed window of early amplitude. In this window, the uncorrelated time resolution changes only
from $44\pm5$\,ns at 435\,V to $30\pm4$\,ns at 490\,V, while for all signal sizes it changes by a factor of about
four over the same voltages. Most of the dependence of the uncorrelated time resolution on the voltage is therefore
a dependence on the size of the signal. The event time resolution in the same window still falls, from $82\pm8$ to
$39\pm4$\,ns. The part of the time resolution that is common to the two views therefore has a dependence on the
voltage that is not explained by the amplitude.
