Micromegas in the n_TOF DAQ: gamma-flash response, recovery and charge

Dylan Neff · 11 August 2026 · n_TOF EAR2 run 224709 (9 August, detector-A HV scan) · July runs 224302 / 224325 / 224327 in Appendix A

The gamma flash puts about a thousand times the DREAM front end's full-scale input charge onto a single strip. Measured directly, on strip 32 of MX17 detector A, cable Y8, digitised at 1 GS/s with no charge-sensitive preamplifier anywhere in the chain: 668 pC on one strip per dedicated proton pulse at the production operating point, against a DREAM CSA full scale of 600 fC — a factor 1,113. That is the answer to how much charge we can swallow before we die: not this much, and not by a small margin.

Two independent instruments agree on the shape to a few per cent. The same charge measured through the resistive layer's HV supply current — a different electrode, a different instrument, nine orders of magnitude less bandwidth — was run over the same 25 working points. Point by point the two differ by a constant: the chamber charge divided by the single-strip charge is 266 ± 15 strips-equivalent across the whole scan, a spread of 5.7 % over a factor 21 in charge. Both also see the response compress with intensity, by the same few per cent per proton.

The absolute scales close too, once the board is read from its gerbers rather than assumed. The readout is not two planes of solid strips: it is a 512 × 512 pad grid, checkerboard-bussed, so a uniform flash splits exactly 50/50 between the X and Y views and 85 % of the resistive-layer charge images onto the pad plane. That accounting turns the HV-current charge into a uniform expectation of 131 pC per strip — and the measured strip carries 4.2 ± 0.3× that, one constant across all 25 working points. The strip's own intensity compression, read through the board's sheet capacitance, independently measures the local flash density at 2.9× the chamber average — so the factor is real, local, and physical: the delivered charge density at this strip is three-to-four times the chamber average. Whether that is the flash illumination profile or a local gain enhancement near the board edge, one channel cannot say — but the two instruments agree wherever they measure the same thing.

Gain cannot rescue it, and the dead time follows. Fitting the flash into 600 fC would need 169 V less amplification, where a real track is equally invisible. Draining 668 pC through the CSA's 9–90 nA feedback takes 7–74 ms — which is the millisecond dead time DREAM shows, derived from an independent charge measurement.

The chamber itself is fine: it is clear within microseconds and taking hits again at 30 µs, the first instant the DAQ allows. That evidence is in Appendix D.

The measurement: a detector-A scan on one strip

On 9 August a micromegas input was patched into the n_TOF DAQ for three runs, 224708–224710. The connection is known: MMA = strip 32 of MX17 detector A, cable Y8 — a single strip, not the mesh, digitised at 1 GS/s over the full 20 ms neutron cycle with no charge-sensitive preamplifier anywhere in the chain. That last point is what makes this measurement possible at all: every other view of the flash we have goes through the DREAM front end, which the flash saturates. The input range was set to 5,044 mV full scale (77.0 µV per count) with the baseline parked near zero (-14 mV); single-sample noise is 1.41 mV.

224709 (17:05–19:38, 344 files, 1.5 TB) contains a detector-A-only drift × amplification scan, 17:10–19:31, in 25 plateaus of ~5.7 min. B, C and D were held fixed throughout. That matters twice over: it removes the degeneracy that made the July chamber assignment impossible, and because the flash charge moves by more than an order of magnitude while only A's voltages move, it independently confirms the channel is on detector A. Each plateau is analysed after a 45 s settling cut; 1567 dedicated and 1091 parasitic bunches are used, split on the beam-pickup amplitude.

The drift field does not matter here

Compared at the same amplification voltage, the three drift settings agree to within 10.6 % (typically 2–8 %), with no systematic ordering — the three curves in the figure lie on top of each other over a sixteen-fold range of charge. Read this as a bound rather than a null measurement: the drift branches were taken one after another, and the 570 V point, revisited three times over an hour, fell 4.7 % across those visits. The slow drift in time and the apparent drift-field spread are the same size, so they cannot be separated in this run. What can be said is that there is no strong drift-field dependence between 500 and 700 V — in contrast to July's 224327, where 600 → 800 V gave +37 % on a different chamber and a different gas.

The charge on one strip

Everything here is quoted as a charge. The conversion is Q = (Δt/R) ∑ ΔV, which for this channel comes to 1.5392 fC per count·ns into 50 Ω; the arithmetic is in Appendix A and the 10–20 m of BNC costs under 1 % of it, which is shown in Appendix B.

All of it refers to one strip: MMA is strip 32 of detector A's Y plane, one of 512 strips at 0.778 mm pitch, in a chamber that has the same again on the X plane. The gamma flash illuminates the whole chamber; this is what landed on a 0.78 mm slice of it, and scaling that back up to the chamber is what the comparison below is about.

The integration runs over 11–20 µs, which covers the whole positive lobe: the 20–30 µs window that follows carries −8 % of it in July and −12 % in the other July run — the slow return of the AC coupling, not signal. The running integral below flattens well before the window closes, which is the check that nothing is being cut off.

The spread, bunch to bunch, on one strip

At a fixed working point the flash charge on strip 32 still varies from bunch to bunch. Three things contribute, and they can be separated: the proton intensity jitters within a class (the beam pickup measures it), the electronics contribute a fixed additive term, and what is left is the detector.

σ2Q / Q2 = (σI/I)2 + (σnoise/Q)2 + (σdet/Q)2

The electronics term is measured, not modelled: integrating a 9 µs window of baseline before the flash, with the same formula, gives a noise-equivalent charge of 23.5 pC RMS. That is 8.8× larger than the 2.67 pC expected from the 1.41 mV single-sample noise if it were white — the baseline wanders coherently, so it integrates up rather than averaging down. That is the floor on any single-bunch charge measurement here.

A completely independent measurement: the HV supply current

A single strip on a 1 GS/s digitiser is one way to weigh the flash. There is a second, which shares nothing with it — not the electrode, not the instrument, not the bandwidth — and which was worked out in parallel (ntof_july_analysis/flash_charge/). It is worth walking through, because the comparison below is only as good as one's confidence in both halves.

In one line: the avalanche ions land on the resistive layer, so the current the HV supply delivers is the avalanche charge. The monitor samples at ~1 Hz while beam pulses arrive every ~3.4 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage — the median is the leakage at that exact voltage and the mean minus that median is the beam-induced part:

Qpulse = ⟨I⟩ − Ileak fpulse why a 1 Hz monitor can weigh a millisecond burst, and the three validations, are in Appendix C

The parallel package established the method on July data. Here it is applied point for point to the same 25 plateaus as the waveform scan, on the same detector, day and gas — so nothing has to be transported between the two measurements. The pulse rate is counted from the n_TOF side, using the wall clock each bunch of 224709 carries, rather than from the beam log.

One feature of that trace deserves a comment: the standing baseline jumps at every drift-HV move — from 16 to 587 nA when the drift went 700 → 600 V, and by ~0.15–0.2 µA at each later move — then relaxes over tens of minutes (the first branch shows the same relaxation from the pre-scan ramp, 60 → 16 nA). The direction rules out a resistive path from the cage divider: the drift voltage went down and the resist baseline went up. It is a charging/relaxation current from moving the cathode, and it is benign for the charge estimate by construction: the per-plateau median subtracts it, its worst within-plateau movement is 24 nA and roughly linear (a linear baseline drift cancels in mean − median to first order), and the bootstrap error bars absorb what remains. It is also, incidentally, why a single global baseline would not work — the per-plateau median is doing real work here.

At the shared working point (700 / 540 V) it gives 143 nC per beam pulse over the whole chamber — which also reproduces the independent morning measurement of the same setpoint from run_158, 142 ± 3 nC, to better than 1 %.

The two measurements compared

These are very different quantities — one strip's prompt pulse over a microsecond against a whole chamber's avalanche charge integrated over the entire cycle — so they cannot simply be set equal. They can be compared on three axes, and all three are worth having because they fail in different ways.

1. The gain slope — calibration-free

Neither the 50 Ω assumption nor the monitor's absolute scale enters the shape of charge against amplification voltage. This is the cleanest test, and it requires believing nothing about either instrument's calibration:

2. Scaling one strip to the whole chamber — what the board actually is

To put the two on the same axis, the single-strip charge has to be scaled to the chamber. The first version of this note did that with a strip count and an assumption about X/Y sharing. The board design — gerbers, stack-up and solved electrostatics, all in the response-simulation repository (~/CLionProjects/MX17_Geant) — replaces both with measured facts, and they change the picture:

  • There are no strips. The readout is a 512 × 512 grid of 680 µm pads on 780 µm pitch, bussed in a checkerboard: a “Y strip” is a comb of 256 pads on 1.56 mm pitch along a row (every second pad), and the intervening pads belong to X combs running the other way. X and Y live in the same copper plane. For a uniform flash the two views therefore split the image charge exactly 50/50, by symmetry — the “all of it could appear on the Y plane” reading is not an option the hardware offers.
  • 85 % of the charge on the resistive layer images onto the pad plane (solved from the real stack: 50 µm coverlay + 19 µm glue against a 150 µm mesh gap, with the inter-pad channels floating); the mesh takes the rest.
  • The resistive layer is itself strips — 550 µm screen-printed resistive lines on 800 µm pitch running along y, contacting copper buses at the two y-ends of the active area and nothing in between. At the frozen 2 MΩ/sq that drain takes τ ≈ 17 ms: on the 9 µs integration window the sheet is charge-conserving, and the supply current is the whole per-pulse charge delivered a second later — the two instruments cannot double-count or miss a loop between them.
  • The Y-edge passivation (18.0 / 18.7 mm, measured on this chamber) leaves 465 of 512 Y combs live, and shortens every X comb to 90.8 % of its length — 465 full-length-equivalent channels per view.

The uniform-illumination expectation for one live Y comb is then a number, not a range:

Qstrip = Qchamber × 0.85 × ½ / 465 = 143 nC × 0.91‰ = 131 pC 0.85 = image capture of the pad plane (W2 boundary; 0.84–0.85 depending on solver detail) · ½ = the checkerboard's exact view split · 465 live Y combs · at the 700/540 working point

The shape agreement is half the result. Point by point, the measured strip charge over the uniform-share expectation is 4.2 ± 0.3 across all 25 plateaus — a spread of 6.2 % over a factor 21 in charge and three drift settings. Two instruments sharing nothing agree on the shape of the response to a few per cent; what is left is a single constant, and the board accounting has sharpened it: it is now a genuine factor four, and it must be explained, not absorbed.

What cannot carry the factor

  • Anything passive in the signal path. The strip is a capacitive current source: every series element (cable, back-termination, connector) passes its whole charge to a 50 Ω load, and every shunt (splitter, attenuator, a second termination, a still-connected DREAM input) makes the true strip charge larger than measured. Passive patch errors can only widen the gap. The one instrumental escape is a digitiser input well above 50 Ω — which the pulse shape argues against, and a pulser through the same patch would settle.
  • Lateral charge spreading. For uniform illumination, what diffuses out of the comb's band diffuses in (4.2 mm in the window at the frozen resistivity); conservation makes the average share kernel-independent.
  • X/Y sharing. Exactly ½ by the checkerboard's symmetry.
  • A missing current loop. The drift supply was checked across the same scan: its monitor shows pure divider-current steps tracking the drift setting and no beam response at all, so the avalanche charge all flows through the monitored resist channel.
  • The saturated neighbours. During the flash every DREAM-connected comb rails within nanoseconds, and one could worry the lone 50 Ω channel drains its neighbours' image through the capacitive network. The scale rules it out: a railed input floats by at most a volt before its protection conducts, while the resistive layer under 10 V of local flash charge is what drives the division — the redistribution is a ≤10 % effect, not a factor.
  • The neutron tail. The supply integrates the whole cycle, the lobe only 9 µs — so any tail charge makes the true residual larger, not smaller. On the strip's own record the above-threshold tail beyond 30 µs is zero at the working point and 1–2 % at the hottest scan point.

What does carry it: the strip itself reports that its neighbourhood is crowded

First, the honest baseline, stated without any interpretation: with the board accounting and the assumption of a uniform response, the waveform and the supply current disagree by one constant factor of 4.2. Everything in this subsection is evidence about where that factor lives — specifically, that it is charge really arriving near this strip, not a broken calibration. The evidence is a nonlinearity the strip itself displays, and the argument has three steps:

Step 1 — arriving charge lowers the very voltage that amplifies it. The resistive layer is one plate of a capacitor (c′ = 0.50 µF per m² of area, computed from the board stack), and during the flash it cannot drain — the path to the HV supply takes ~17 ms. So when the flash dumps charge onto some region of it, that region's voltage drops, by exactly the capacitor law: ΔV = (charge per area) / c′. But this is the amplification voltage, and the gas gain depends on it exponentially — every 22 V is a factor e (measured on this same scan). Put together: during a flash, charge that arrives late is amplified less than charge that arrived early, because the early charge has already pulled the voltage down. The denser the charge landing in a region, the more of this self-suppression that region shows.

Step 2 — the beam runs a controlled “double the input” experiment every 36 s. Dedicated pulses carry 2.00× the protons of parasitic ones (measured pulse by pulse with the beam pickup). If amplification were linear, dedicated pulses would give exactly 2.00× the charge. Measured on strip 32 at the working point they give 1.85× — a 7.6 % shortfall per proton. Note what this shortfall is: a ratio of two measurements on the same strip. Every linear calibration cancels out of a ratio — the 50 Ω assumption, the capture fractions, the cable, all of it. Whatever else might be wrong with our absolute scales, the 7.6 % is real.

Step 3 — the size of the shortfall is a ruler for the local density. Run Step 1 backwards. To produce a 7.6 % shortfall between ×1 and ×2 pulses, the parasitic flash must pull the layer down by about 4 V in the region where this strip's charge is amplified — directly above it. By the capacitor law, 4 V of sag at c′ = 0.50 µF/m² means about 194 pC/cm² landed there. Now compare: the supply current says the chamber-average parasitic flash is 66 pC/cm². If this strip's neighbourhood received only that average, the sag would be 1.3 V and the shortfall 3 % — the measured 7.6 % excludes it. So the strip's own nonlinearity, read with nothing but the board capacitance and the measured gain slope, says its neighbourhood received ~3× the chamber-average density. The waveform lobe — the absolute route, with the 50 Ω and capture calibrations — says 274 pC/cm², i.e. ~4×. Two routes that share no calibration, the same answer to ~40 %:

The scan turns this one-point argument into a curve. Raising the amplification voltage raises the charge per pulse by a factor 21 across the scan — and therefore the density, and therefore the predicted shortfall, which grows along a definite curve with nothing fitted: density from the lobe, capacitance from the board, gain slope from the scan itself. The measured shortfalls ride that curve from 8 % at the working point up to ~29 % at the hottest point:

What the density factor is made of, this note cannot say. The delivered density is (primary ionisation per area) × (local gas gain), so a 3–4× excess can be an illumination profile — the flash is prompt γs and their conversions, and the material around the active area (frame, support plate, board edge) serves an edge strip differently from the middle — or a local gain enhancement: this strip sits 5–7 mm from the passivation edge, where amplification-field distortion is expected and where the June bench work found spark activity concentrated. Both are multiplicative constants, both are consistent with the clean e-fold slope (a locally higher gain shifts the exponential without bending it), and one channel cannot separate them. What would: the DREAM recovery-time map across the plane (a gain hot-spot is fixed to the detector; an illumination profile follows the beam), a second patched strip near the centre, or the June bench amplitude maps of this very chamber at this strip's position.

One instrument on a single strip, one on a whole chamber's supply, independently seeing the same few-to-fifteen percent shortfall. That makes space charge in the amplification region a good deal more credible than it was from either alone.

A natural question is whether the supply current can make the dedicated/parasitic split on this same scan, point for point with the waveform. It cannot — and the reason is timing, not statistics. There are plenty of parasitic pulses (36 at the working point alone); the problem is that a parasitic pulse is never alone. The monitor smears every pulse into a ~1–2 s bump and timestamps its samples in whole seconds, so a pulse can only be read out individually when no other pulse sits within a few seconds of it — and the PS supercycle delivers parasitic pulses 1.2–2.4 s from their neighbours, always, so their bumps always overlap with someone else's. Only dedicated pulses, which border the supercycle's long gaps, ever stand alone: a strict isolation cut on this run keeps 91 dedicated and 1 parasitic of 2 672. (The clock offset itself resolves cleanly, +2 s; and fitting the overlapping bumps instead of cutting them is the ill-conditioning trap documented in the July handoff, §8.6.) The split has been made with the supply current where the 1 s time base was reconstructed to millisecond accuracy — run_79 at the production point, the det A / det C rows in the table above — and that is the imon's answer: same sign, same size as the waveform's.

The pulse mix does not, however, drag the comparison anywhere: the strip-to-chamber ratio is taken mix-against-mix — both instruments average the same bunches — so the 4.2 is mix-independent. And the mix can be separated algebraically, using the counted classes and the waveform-measured charge ratio: at the working point the chamber delivers 177 nC per dedicated pulse and 96 nC per parasitic pulse, which are the numbers to quote when a single-class figure is needed rather than the 143 nC mix average.

What this means for DREAM

The DREAM charge-sensitive amplifier has selectable input ranges of 50 / 100 / 200 / 600 fC. Taking the largest:

The flash puts roughly a thousand times the front end's full-scale input charge onto a single strip. That is the answer to “how much can we swallow before we die”: not this much, and not by a small margin. The chamber-average figure from the HV current with the board accounting, 219×, is the optimistic bound — a typical channel away from the hot region; the strip actually measured sits at 1,113×. And 600 fC is the largest of the four selectable ranges: a parallel audit of 44 saved bench configs reads the DREAM registers as 200 fC, which if it holds for the beam runs makes every multiple here three times worse.

Gain cannot fix it. With the measured e-folding of 24 V, bringing 668 pC down to 600 fC takes ln(1,113) × 24 V = 169 V less amplification — an operating point near 371 V, where the gain for an actual track is also down by a factor of a thousand and there is nothing left to read. The flash and the physics scale together; the amplification knob does not separate them.

And the dead time falls out of it. A pinned CSA recovers by draining the accumulated charge through its feedback, which the saturation note puts at 9–90 nA. Q/Ifb for 668 pC is 7.4 to 74 ms — which brackets the millisecond-scale dead time DREAM actually shows, from an independent measurement of the charge, and agrees with the parallel result that dead time follows delivered charge as t ∝ Q1.2 across three chambers. Appendix D shows the chamber itself is clear within microseconds, so all of that millisecond structure lives in the front end.

What this does not establish

  • Which chamber the July runs are. All four MX17 chambers step in lockstep there — every HV state is a joint (A,B,C,D) setting and each ladder spans the same 20 V — so the July flash charge correlates identically with all four. The August run settles its own case (detector A, strip 32, cable Y8, corroborated by the A-only scan), but it does not retroactively identify the July channel: `MMB` was live then, `MMA` now, and the input range changed by ten times in between.
  • The DREAM side was not re-measured here. This work shows the chamber recovers in microseconds. It does not itself demonstrate what the DREAM preamplifier, shaper or zero-suppression do with the same charge — that comparison is the obvious next step, and DREAM run_18 is simultaneous with 224327, so it can be done on the same bunches.
  • The absolute charge scale assumes a direct 50 Ω termination and no amplification between chamber and digitiser. Note the direction: every passive error in that path — a splitter, an attenuator, a second termination, a still-connected DREAM input — makes the true strip charge larger than quoted, never smaller. Only a digitiser input well above 50 Ω would lower it, which the pulse shape argues against. A pulser through the same patch would pin the whole chain and is the single most valuable follow-up measurement. The 10–20 m of RG-58 is not a worry — it costs under 1 % of the charge, and that is shown rather than assumed above.
  • Why the density is high at this strip is inferred, not mapped. The board accounting plus the compression establish that the residual factor 4.2 is real local charge density — but density is illumination × local gain, and one channel cannot split the product. Four measurements would: (1) the per-channel recovery-time map from DREAM run_160/161, simultaneous with this scan — dead time follows delivered charge as t ∝ Q1.2, so the saturation-recovery pattern across the plane is a flash-charge profile read from data that already exist, and a gain hot-spot is fixed to the detector while an illumination profile follows the beam; (2) the June cosmic-bench amplitude maps of this very chamber at this strip's position, which bound the gain component alone; (3) a second patched strip near the chamber centre; (4) the pulser calibration above, which removes the one instrumental alternative. The two local densitometers also differ by ~40 % (4.1 vs 2.9), which is attributed to the roughness of the sheet-charging model and not further explained here.

Appendix A — how the charge is computed

1. Codes to volts

The digitiser is a 16-bit S014 at 1 GS/s. Samples are signed int16. For bunch k the baseline b is the median of the first 2 µs, taken per bunch so that slow baseline movement does not leak into the integral, and the signal is negative-going, so the deviation is

ΔVi = ( b − ci ) · VFS216 = ( b − ci ) × 76.96 µV VFS = 5,043.79 mV (the channel's own full scale, from the run's MODH record), 216 = 65,536 codes

2. Volts to charge

The digitiser input is a 50 Ω termination, so the sampled voltage is the strip current through that resistor, v(t) = R i(t). The charge is the time integral of the current, which at a fixed sampling interval is just a sum:

Q = ∫ i(t) dt = 1R ∫ v(t) dt  ≈  ΔtR ∑i ΔVi

Collecting the constants gives the number used throughout:

Δt · VFS R · 216 = 1.5392 fC per count·ns Δt = 1 ns, R = 50 Ω. The July runs, on a ten-times finer range, give 0.1537 fC per count·ns.

Appendix C — the HV-current method in detail

The idea

Every electron avalanche in the amplification gap ends with its ions drifting to the resistive layer. That charge has to be resupplied by the HV power supply. So the average current the supply delivers is the avalanche charge, integrated over everything the beam pulse does — and it is measured outside the readout chain entirely, by the CAEN crate's own current monitor, which was logged once a second for every sub-run of the campaign. No new data, no reprocessing.

Qpulse = ⟨I⟩ − Ileak fpulse ⟨I⟩ = mean of the monitor over a sub-run, Ileak = its median, fpulse = beam pulses per second in that sub-run's own time window

Why the median is the leakage

This is the trick that makes it work across an HV scan. The monitor samples at ~1 Hz while beam pulses arrive every ~3.3 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage current. The sub-run median is therefore the leakage at that exact voltage — self-calibrating, which matters because leakage is itself strongly HV-dependent and one subtracted constant would not do. The mean minus that median is the beam-induced part.

Why a 1 Hz monitor can see a 10 ms burst

The obvious objection is that a once-a-second reading cannot catch a millisecond burst, so the mean would underestimate by a large and unknown factor. That was tested and closed by measuring the monitor's impulse response directly, phase-folding the current against individual beam pulses. The readback is a ~1 s averager, not a snapshot: the response to one pulse rises from 0.3 s, peaks at 88 nA around 1.1 s, is back to zero by 2.3 s, and its area equals the charge. Two timestamp-free checks bound it without using any clock — 26.6 % of samples sit above baseline where an instantaneous reader would give 0.3 %, and the largest single-sample excess implies an averaging window ≥ 0.47 s against a burst of milliseconds. A smoothing filter conserves its integral, so the sample mean recovers the time-average current: these are measurements, not lower bounds, and there is no correction factor.

It passes the tests that matter: zero on a true beam-off run (run_159, 0.000 Hz, even on a channel carrying 2.9 µA of leakage), the same charge per pulse at a 10× different beam rate (run_157 against run_158), and four independent estimators — mean−median, detrended, isolated-pulse fold area, least-squares deconvolution — agreeing to ±2.5 %.

Appendix D — July 2026: the flash is not a detector dead time

This is the earlier half of the study, kept in full. Three runs from 5–9 July carried a micromegas channel whose cabling was never recorded, so nothing here can be attached to a named chamber — but they answer a different question from the scan above, and they answer it well: the chamber is not paralysed by the flash. The signal returns below a 4 mV threshold 0.87 µs after its own peak, hits resume at 30.3 µs (the first instant the DAQ allows one) at the highest rate of the whole 20 ms cycle, and a genuine beam-off run shows the acquisition itself is flat over four decades of time. The millisecond dead time is a DREAM-chain property.

Appendix E — decoding integrity

The user-facing worry with this raw format is sign. The samples are int16_t — ntoflib declares std::vector<int16_t> data in ReaderStructACQC.h, and the n_TOF PSA reads them that way. Decoded signed, nothing wraps:

  • 56,647,470 samples checked across both beam runs
  • 0 at the positive rail, 0 at the negative rail
  • 0 sample-to-sample steps above 20 000 counts — the wrap signature — with the largest step 5,883 counts

The zero-suppression fill code is -32768, bit-identical to the negative rail, and is masked rather than integrated. A block’s payload begins 259 samples before its stated start.
