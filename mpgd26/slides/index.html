Status · What we came for
Almost all of the X17 rate is in the MeV
³He(n,γ) capture → internal pair conversion → X17, per decade of neutron energy, for a nominal 500 bar cell. Two decades carry 79 % of the rate — and n_TOF sorts them for us by time of flight, so they arrive 0.45–4.5 µs after the γ flash. The thermal end of the beam is the other measurement: 1000× later, and 7.5× less rate.
Rate calculation for a nominal 40 mm, 500 bar ³He cell (data/x17_rate_3He.txt, Dec 2025): EAR2 flux, ³He(n,γ) capture, 2.1×10−3 IPC per capture, 2.5×10−2 X17 per IPC pair; flight time is the relativistic conversion over 19.5 m. A nominal per-day rate, not a yield projection for this run.
Status · The environment
Our read-out is DREAM — and the γ flash rails every channel of it
  • DREAM — Dead-timeless Read-out Electronics ASIC for Micromegas, developed at CEA Saclay and built for the CLAS12 Micromegas vertex tracker.
  • 512 channels per front-end unit (8 ASICs × 64); two units per chamber, so 1 024 channels of MX17 each.
  • Every channel: a charge-sensitive preamplifier and shaper, full scale switchable 50–600 fC, into a switched-capacitor analogue memory.
  • Sampled here at 20 ns, digitised on an external trigger — the chip is designed to run continuously and never stop.
  • February 2026, the first prototype on beam: saturated — and saturated in a way that hides itself. The ADC rails to +4095, crosses to 0, and settles onto a flat baseline within ~3 µs.
One flash, the 24 hottest strips — run_32, flash compensation off
The chip is not the problem and neither is the gain — a channel with 10²–10³ times its full-scale charge on the input has nowhere to put it. And that flat baseline is a lie: before the flash the traces carry visible noise; after it they do not. A preamplifier pinned against its rail is static — the AC-coupled shaper downstream returns to pedestal, but the channel has no small-signal gain, so it shows neither tracks nor noise. The absence of noise is the only tell at ADC level, and it is what the backup panel measures.
DREAM: C. Flouzat et al., CEA Saclay — the front end of the CLAS12 Micromegas vertex tracker, and of these chambers. Channel and range counts from the chip manual and the run configuration. The finding is February 2026, on the first prototype; the trace drawn is a July recording of the same behaviour (run_32, flash compensation off, resist 500 V, 400 samples × 20 ns) — February’s read-out windows were too short to hold the whole excursion. The mechanism: the CSA stays pinned while the input current exceeds its ~9–90 nA feedback limit.

The same flash, seen by both chains — aligned on their own peaks
One strip of detector A was cabled to the n_TOF DAQ and digitised directly at 1 GS/s with no charge-sensitive preamplifier. At the production amplification voltage its flash signal is a 45 mV pulse that is back under the 4 mV threshold 2.0 µs after its own peak. Through DREAM, at the same 540 V, the same chamber does not have its noise back for 5.0 ms. Nothing in the gas, the field or the amplification stage has a millisecond time constant — the dead time belongs to the charge-integrating front end, and only to that.
Green, both panels: run 224709 (9 Aug), MMA = strip 32 of det A on cable Y8, Ar/iso 90/10, drift 700 / amplification 540 V; bunch-mean of 52 dedicated pulses, recovery = last sample above 4 mV after the trace’s own peak. Summary
Summary — and what the data are for
  • Saclay resistive micro-TPCs measure a track direction to ~1°, from a waveform-first forward fit that models the resistive sharing instead of fighting it
  • A four-chamber tracker ran the full 2026 X17 campaign at n_TOF EAR2: 41.8 M events, 17.9 TB, 44 days — the dataset is complete
  • The γ flash saturates the charge-integrating front end for milliseconds, so what we recorded is the thermal window — the MeV rate is inside that dead time

The EAR2 render, and what in it is drawn rather than measured
Left — the in-house render (mpgd26/make_ear2.py + scenes_ear2.py; readable at full size, with live text, in figures/ear2_beamline_light_labelled.pdf): one drawing in five frames (main flow, slides 5–9), same camera and same scale, cut open along the beam axis, with one break that removes ~20 m of empty pipe — so the labelled heights are the real ones and the drawing’s are not. What the break hides: the first collimator at 7.4 m (1 m of iron, 200 mm bore), the sweeping magnet at 10.4 m and the filter station at 11.4 m, all ~10 m below the EAR2 floor. It stops about 1.15 m above the station: the bunker ceiling (23.66 m) and the beam dump standing on the roof (entrance 24.73 m — a borated-polyethylene core inside 1.6 m of iron inside 3.2 m of concrete) are real and above the frame, and the wide pipe heading there is cut off by the frame rather than ended. The lower pipe really does end about a metre above the floor; the space above it is open hall, and the beam crosses it in air. Four things are drawn rather than sourced. (i) The shape of the line inside the hall — the white shielding on the floor, the stepped diameters, the reducer and the height at which the upper pipe starts — is scaled off the photograph on the right against its ~1.2 m white disc, and is good for “it changes diameter here, about this much” and no finer. (ii) The lead disks are a 0.36 m stack against the 0.57 m effective height in the design paper — the drawn hall segment is 1 m tall and also has to carry the collar, the reducer and the exit flange. (iii) The station’s support frame is left out, so the capsule appears to float; it does not, and the photograph shows the aluminium-profile frame it really hangs in. (iv) Two of the four chambers are drawn, in section, and their drawn azimuths (136° apart) are chosen to present that section to this camera — the real station has four, 90° apart, in a pinwheel around the sample. Drawn as the true pinwheel the two solid arms come out 45° to the screen and close on the capsule from both sides until they touch it; the four-arm version is rendered too, as figures/ear2_onfig_5_station_4arm.png. Nothing at the station is drawn 1:1: the ³He capsule is 23 mm across against a 600 mm target, so at true scale it is one pixel, and it is drawn 5.5× oversize; the chambers are drawn 1.35× oversize (board, gap and frame scaled together, so each is still a chamber and only its size is wrong) and draw 24 strips rather than 512, the real 0.78 mm pitch being a quarter of a pixel here. What ε is
εf is the X-charge of fermion f, in units of the electron charge — the photon couples to every charged particle with the one strength e; the X couples to each fermion with its own small εf·e, and the pattern of those charges is what the model gets to choose. Rates go as ε², so ε ~ 10−3 means ~10−6 of an electromagnetic rate — why a 17 MeV particle can still be unexcluded. In our channel: production ∝ εn² (with εp ≈ 0, next slide), decay to e⁺e⁻ needs εe ≠ 0 and its lifetime ∝ 1/εe² — which is exactly the handle beam-dump experiments pull on. Why “protophobic” — and the coupling window still open
The trapa generic 17 MeV vector coupled to quarks is already dead: NA48/2's π⁰ → γ(X→e⁺e⁻) search would have seen it. The π⁰ amplitude ∝ (2εu+εd) = εp, the coupling to protons
The escapemake εp nearly vanish — “protophobic”: |εp| ≲ 1.2×10−3, while the ⁸Be rate wants |εn| = (2–10)×10−3. A boson that sees neutrons but not protons kills the π⁰ constraint by construction; n–Pb scattering allows |εn| up to 2.5×10−2
εe floorit must also couple to electrons (it decays to them). Too weak and it lives long enough to escape a beam dump: SLAC E141 requires |εe| > 2×10−4; NA64 (100 GeV e⁻ on active target, X→e⁺e⁻) raised that floor stepwise, excluding 1.2×10−4 < εe < 6.8×10−4 by 2020
εe ceilingtoo strong and it shifts the electron g−2 and shows in KLOE-2 e⁺e⁻→γX: |εe| ≲ 1.4×10−3
What survives|εe| ≈ (0.7–1.4)×10−3 — a relatively strongly coupled, promptly decaying X (cτ ~ mm). NA64 squeezes from below; each run narrows the window rather than closing it

Why a per-strip hit time is not a drift time
Four estimators — rising edge, CFD, peak, full matched filter — on the same S: late at the mesh, early at the cathode. The ladder is compressed 13–15 % for the best offline estimators, 20–30 % for the production threshold time
The signature: v implied by the hits ladder depends on track angle (spread 13 µm/ns); the forward fit is flat at the calibrated 36.6
Each strip’s waveform is its own charge plus delayed, dispersed copies of its neighbours’ (slide 9). On an inclined track those copies come from different depths, so any single time per strip times a mixture — a matched filter, being optimal for one pulse shape, returns exactly the biased weighted mean. Both ends of the ladder squeeze inward; read with the true drift velocity, a compressed ladder is a track ~4° too steep. Estimator-independent, so no threshold change fixes it — the mixing has to be modelled forward. House rule ever since: no angle is believed until the implied-velocity flatness check passes. sat_det3, 320 reference-matched events; docs/wft_reference Part II.

Calibration: pin the geometry to the reference, measure the response
The impulse response is measured, not assumed: bright unsaturated strips of clearly inclined tracks (their own charge arrives within ~1.5 samples), aligned on the 50 % crossing, median-stacked — per plane, because Y undershoots several times deeper than X
The trap that cost more time than everything else: the calibration χ² over (sharing amplitude, v) is a long shallow valley — a 10 % move in v costs < 1 % of χ², yet becomes 10 % on every angle via tan θ = w/v. A free fit slides up the valley; better optimisers just find the wrong bottom faster
Calibration is the one stage allowed to see the M3 reference — “given the track, what is the response?” — reconstruction never is, or every efficiency and alignment number would be circular. The degeneracy is broken by pinning to external measurements: the sharing kernel from direct stacking of near-normal tracks (needs no model and no v; beam-verified invariant across drift field, gas and a 6 % gain step — a property of the resistive layer, not the operating point), and v from a drift scan, the implied-v plateau or Magboltz. Free-fit pathologies announce themselves: c₁ → 0 with kY blown up and σp0 inflated means a degenerate calibration, not a detector property. “Why not just deconvolve the sharing?”
The thing un-sharing must resolve is the neighbour copy: a few-percent amplitude, RC-low-passed, crossing the noise floor at ~2–3 MHz — 11–12 of the window’s 17 frequency modes carry no copy information at all. A naive inverse amplifies noise ×30 exactly there; regularisation suppresses the blow-up but cannot manufacture the missing information
Measured, same 250 events, only t₀ borrowed from the fit: ensemble medians track the diagonal, but per event the deconvolved angle scatters σ68 = 2.8° against the forward fit’s 1.0°, with a bimodal residual
It was tried, and the honest verdict is a boundary, not a verdict: deconvolution is stable enough to display, too unstable to measure from. Ensemble-averaged it is fine — the line-free 3-D event displays behind the threading validation are themselves a regularised 2-D deconvolution, kept precisely so that validation would not be circular. What fails is deconvolution as a per-event geometry estimator. The forward direction never inverts anything: generating a prediction is always well-conditioned, and the same measured kernel appears on both sides of the comparison. How the charge per pulse is measured
Qpulse = [ mean(Iresist) − median(Iresist) ] / fpulse  — per sub-run, per chamber
  • The resistive-layer supply carries the avalanche ion current, so its average current is the charge delivered to the amplification stage, integrated over the whole pulse. It sits entirely outside the readout chain.
  • The readback samples at ~1 Hz while beam pulses arrive every ~3.3 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage: the median is the leakage at that exact HV, and mean − median is the beam-induced part. Self-calibrating, which is what makes an HV scan usable.
  • Pulse rate comes per sub-run from the beam-intensity log, not from a run average.

Backup · How the charge number survives scrutiny
Why that is a measurement and not a comforting story
  • What imon physically is. The resistive layer is what the avalanche ions land on. Every electron the gas multiplies has to be resupplied through that one HV channel, so the channel's average current is the avalanche charge per second — divided by the pulse rate, the charge per pulse. It sits entirely outside the readout, which is the whole point: the readout is the thing that saturates.
  • Why mean − median. The readback samples at ~1 Hz and beam pulses come every ~3.3 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage current. The median is therefore the leakage at that exact voltage — and leakage is strongly HV-dependent, which is why a single subtracted constant would not do and why an HV scan is usable at all. The mean includes the beam. The difference is beam-induced current; divided by the pulse rate, it is charge per pulse.
  • Where the doubt came from. The burst lasts milliseconds; the samples are a second apart. A monitor that reported an instantaneous current would sit at baseline almost always and catch a burst once in ~350 samples — and every charge we quote would have been a lower bound by a factor nobody had measured.
  • The two numbers that settle it, using no timestamps at all. 27 % of samples sit above baseline, not 0.3 %. And the largest single-sample excess ever recorded on that channel is 0.216 µA, where an instantaneous read of the same charge in 10 ms would have to read 10.1 µA — 47× larger. Turn that round: a sample that contains a whole burst and reads 0.216 µA must be averaging over at least Q/ΔI = 0.47 s. Both discriminate by two orders of magnitude, in the same direction.
  • And the trap we had to beat. The HV logger writes whole-second timestamps but its loop runs at 1.0162 s, so the true read time drifts through its labelled second and a raw-label fold is smeared by a 1 s box — which would fabricate a wide response from a narrow one. The drift is also the cure: the pattern of dropped seconds over-constrains the logger's own clock, and solving it recovers 95 % of the sample times to 2 ms. The sharp fold is what the figure shows.

Backup · Outlook
What we take away
  • Resistive micro-TPCs work in a spallation γ-flash environment. Measured, not asserted: the same chamber on a direct analog channel is usable 0.87 µs after the flash peak. The chambers are not the limitation.
  • The limitation is charge integration in the front end, and it is now characterised as a one-parameter problem: dead time against delivered charge, t ∝ Q1.2, at ~10² nC per pulse per chamber. That is a specification a future front end can be designed against.
  • What did not work: mesh charge injection as a compensation scheme — tuned to kill the positive rail, it simply moves the channel onto the negative one, with no net reduction in railed time.
  • The requirement the analysis now has to meet: ²⁷Al(n,γ) on the capsule dominates what we trigger on, and it separates from signal on total pair energy, not on opening angle — which asks the tracker for σ(p)/p ≲ 30 % per lepton from multiple scattering or range.
  • Next: in-situ calibration of the reconstruction on beam data, four-arm back-projection of the production statistics, and a direct waveform-level charge measurement through the unsaturated single-channel path.
Backup · Outlook
What a post-LS3 measurement needs
  • The measurement has to move to MeV neutron energies, in the first microseconds after the flash. The thermal gate we ran in is background-characterization territory: ²⁷Al(n,γ) on the capsule makes ~96 % of what we trigger on. That window is exactly the one this run measured and could not use.
  • Not the chamber. The same chamber, digitized directly at 1 GS/s with no charge-sensitive preamplifier, is usable 0.87 µs after the flash peak — nothing in the gas, the field or the amplification stage has a millisecond time constant.
  • The front end, stated as a specification: survive 10²–10³ × the CSA's full-scale input charge in one pulse, and be live again within ~1 µs. The curve to design against is measured: t ∝ Q1.2.
Two routesReduce the charge that arrives — actively divert or clamp the input during the flash. Mesh charge injection was tried and only trades one rail for the other; lowering the gain works, and the run_57 recovery map is the exact exchange rate against efficiency.
Or build a front end that can take it. Direct digitization already survives this environment — we ran it, on the n_TOF DAQ, for the whole 20 ms cycle. Scaling it from one channel to a tracker is the engineering problem.
And then the problem after that one: at MeV energies the rate is ~104 above the thermal gate, and it is not established that tracks separate there. The X17 search at PADME uses a similar micromegas TPC and appears to disentangle comparable pile-up — an encouraging precedent, not a proof. This dataset, with a tracker that already images the target, is the right place to answer it.
