Motivation
n + ³He → the n_TOF search channel
Discovery channel: e⁺e⁻ tracks and their opening angle. Neutron capture is overwhelmingly a γ-ray process — a detector that only responds to a charged, back-to-back pair rejects that background by construction, instead of trying to out-shield it.
n_TOF's neutron beam onto a ³He target — an independent nucleus, an independent facility, from the same simplified picture: capture → ⁴He* at 20.58 MeV → γ / IPC / X17. Measuring that angle is what the Micromegas micro-TPC is built to do, next.
Motivation
What the detector has to separate
Ideal case — Geant4 generator truth, before any material. A 17 MeV two-body decay cannot make a small opening angle: it is kinematically confined above 109°, exactly where ATOMKI see their excess, while internal pair conversion piles up at small angle. That separation is the whole measurement — and it is an angle, which is why this needs a tracker. The real capsule material degrades this picture; quantifying that is part of the search.

Status
Where things stand
  • Four chambers have been running at EAR2 since July, on ³He, in the pinwheel — the full trigger, calorimetry and DAQ chain is closed and taking production statistics as of today.
  • The environment, not the detector, is the hard part. The γ flash saturates the front end for milliseconds — measured, mapped against gain, and now against delivered charge.
  • Tracks reconstruct on beam data and point back at the capsule. Waveform-first reconstruction transfers from the cosmic bench to n_TOF.
  • Analysis is ongoing — this is a status report on a campaign that is still taking data, not a result.
running as this talk is given — numbers are 2026-08-09
Status · The environment
What the γ flash does to a DREAM channel
Every channel rails to +4095, swings through to 0, and is back at a flat baseline within ~3 µs. The baseline is a lie: the shaper AC-couples, so a CSA that is pinned against its rail but static looks like DC and the ADC returns to pedestal. A pinned CSA has no small-signal gain — it shows neither tracks nor noise. The absence of noise is the only tell at ADC level, and it is what the right-hand panel measures.
Status · The environment
The chamber is fine. The front end is not.
Three n_TOF runs carried a micromegas channel digitised directly at 1 GS/s by the n_TOF DAQ, with no charge-sensitive preamplifier. There, the flash signal is back below threshold 0.87 µs after its own peak, and hits resume at the first instant the DAQ permits one — at the highest rate of the entire 20 ms cycle. Nothing in the gas, the field or the amplification stage has a millisecond time constant. The millisecond dead time is a property of the charge-integrating front end, and only of that.

Status · The environment
Charge delivered, versus gain
A gas-gain curve measured through the charge the detector delivers rather than the signal it makes — which is the point, because the signal is what saturates. 30 nC at the bottom of the scan, 800 nC at the top: a factor of ~20 over 60 V, on three chambers independently.
Status · The environment
And what it costs: milliseconds of blindness
Flash defines t = 0; a Poisson pulser then fires random triggers across the ~30 ms gate and each one probes whether the front end has its noise back. Recovery runs from under 1 ms to beyond the gate over 60 V — and the shaded band is where the thermal neutrons arrive. The dead time the flash imposes is the same size as the physics window it precedes.
Status · The environment
Dead time is set by charge, not by voltage
Two independent measurements of the same 31 sub-runs — charge from the HV supply current, recovery from the flash-random probe — and three chambers at three different gains fall on one curve. So HV is only the knob; what the front end cannot survive is the charge. Consistent with the mechanism: the CSA's feedback can only sink ~9–90 nA, and a sustained input current above that pins it against the rail for as long as the current lasts.
Status · Operating in it
We chose the operating point off the dead-time map
  • Deliberately below the efficiency plateau. Production runs drift 700 V, resist 520–540 V — picked for milliseconds of recovery, not for gain. Every ~10 V of resist buys a few ms of blindness.
  • Trigger pushed as low as it will go. A threshold × drift × resist scan showed track efficiency rises toward lower plastic threshold in every time window, so we took 0.90 MIP rather than 1.41.
  • Readout window measured, not assumed — 20 samples × 60 ns holds 95 % of the drift charge with the onset at sample 2; the trigger FIFO watermark was set from a measured acceptance-comb uniformity (CV 1.50 → 0.51).

Status · Operating in it
What we record, and when
Reconstructed tracks on beam data, joined event-by-event to the n_TOF scintillator stream. The turn-on at 1 ms is the DREAM gate, not the reconstruction; the bulk of everything we record sits in the first ~10 ms — the same interval the recovery map says the chambers are only partly alive in. That overlap is the experiment's central operational problem, stated in data.
Status · Reconstruction
The tracks point back at the capsule
Waveform-first reconstruction on beam data, coloured by which SiPM wall segment fired — external position truth on a 100 mm granularity, on a lever arm the chamber knows nothing about. The four bundles are 251 mm apart at the wall and 9 mm apart at the target plane; shuffling the segment labels collapses that to 28 mm and 3.2 mm. Ensemble statement — the waist is a convergence of medians, not a per-track pointing resolution.
Status · Outlook
What we take away
  • Resistive micro-TPCs work in a spallation γ-flash environment. Measured, not asserted: the same chamber on a direct analog channel is usable 0.87 µs after the flash peak. The chambers are not the limitation.
  • The limitation is charge integration in the front end, and it is now characterised as a one-parameter problem: dead time against delivered charge, t ∝ Q1.2, at ~10² nC per pulse per chamber. That is a specification a future front end can be designed against.
  • What did not work: mesh charge injection as a compensation scheme — tuned to kill the positive rail, it simply moves the channel onto the negative one, with no net reduction in railed time.
  • The requirement the analysis now has to meet: ²⁷Al(n,γ) on the capsule dominates what we trigger on, and it separates from signal on total pair energy, not on opening angle — which asks the tracker for σ(p)/p ≲ 30 % per lepton from multiple scattering or range.
  • Next: in-situ calibration of the reconstruction on beam data, four-arm back-projection of the production statistics, and a direct waveform-level charge measurement through the unsaturated single-channel path.

How ATOMKI measured it
  • A thin solid target (Li, tritiated Ti, or B) at the centre of a fixed five-arm array of plastic ΔE–E scintillator telescopes
  • Multiwire proportional chambers in front of each arm give a hit position → pair opening angle, not a 3-D track
  • ~7° angular resolution (FWHM), no charge/vertex reconstruction, fixed acceptance set by the five arms
A wider-acceptance, tracking detector — like the Micromegas micro-TPCs in this talk — is the natural next step. J. Gulyás et al., Nucl. Instrum. Methods Phys. Res. A 808, 21 (2016)

How the charge per pulse is measured
Qpulse = [ mean(Iresist) − median(Iresist) ] / fpulse  — per sub-run, per chamber
  • The resistive-layer supply carries the avalanche ion current, so its average current is the charge delivered to the amplification stage, integrated over the whole pulse. It sits entirely outside the readout chain.
  • The readback samples at ~1 Hz while beam pulses arrive every ~3.3 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage: the median is the leakage at that exact HV, and mean − median is the beam-induced part. Self-calibrating, which is what makes an HV scan usable.
  • Pulse rate comes per sub-run from the beam-intensity log, not from a run average.
Null testbeam-off run at the production setpoint, 0.000 Hz of pulses: ΔI = +0.0002 / +0.0005 / −0.0002 / +0.0015 µA on A/B/C/D, against +0.042 µA with beam — including on a channel carrying 2.9 µA of leakage
Rate scalingsame setpoint, 10× different beam rate: ΔI changes 10×, charge per pulse does not (165 vs 142 nC on det A, 128 vs 99 nC on det C)
HV dependencea smooth ×20 rise over 60 V on three chambers — i.e. the gas-gain curve
Readback responsemeasured, not assumed (2026-08-10): phase-folding imon against the individual beam pulses gives the monitor's response to one impulse of charge — it rises over 0.3 s, peaks at 88 nA, and is back to zero by 2.3 s, with an area equal to the charge. A timestamp-free bound puts the averaging window at ≥ 0.47 s against a ~10 ms burst, so the time-average is preserved and the charges are measurements, not lower bounds. Four estimators agree to ±3 %. See the imon backup slide.
Still not calibratedthis shows the readback conserves the integral of a short burst; it does not check the absolute accuracy of its nA scale, which is a board-datasheet question. The exact CAEN card model is recorded in neither repo and the live crate was deliberately not probed.
Backup · How the charge number survives scrutiny
Does the current readback actually see a millisecond burst?
Yes — it averages over ≥ 0.47 s, so it cannot miss one. The charge numbers are measurements, not lower bounds, and there is no correction factor. Four independent estimators agree to ±3 %.
What the left panel is. Every imon sample is tagged with how long it came after the nearest beam pulse, and then averaged in bins of that delay — a phase fold. What comes out is the monitor's response to one known impulse of charge. The area under it is the charge per pulse: 98 nC. The dashed step is the same fold on the logger's raw whole-second timestamps — visibly wider, and rising before the pulse, which is impossible: that is the timestamp smear, and beating it is most of the work. Right panel: the same shape on three chambers once each is divided by its own area — the shape belongs to the monitor, not to a detector — plus a drift-cathode channel on the same crate and the same logger, which stays flat to the last digit.
Backup · How the charge number survives scrutiny
Why that is a measurement and not a comforting story
  • What imon physically is. The resistive layer is what the avalanche ions land on. Every electron the gas multiplies has to be resupplied through that one HV channel, so the channel's average current is the avalanche charge per second — divided by the pulse rate, the charge per pulse. It sits entirely outside the readout, which is the whole point: the readout is the thing that saturates.
  • Why mean − median. The readback samples at ~1 Hz and beam pulses come every ~3.3 s, so most samples sit at the standing leakage current. The median is therefore the leakage at that exact voltage — and leakage is strongly HV-dependent, which is why a single subtracted constant would not do and why an HV scan is usable at all. The mean includes the beam. The difference is beam-induced current; divided by the pulse rate, it is charge per pulse.
  • Where the doubt came from. The burst lasts milliseconds; the samples are a second apart. A monitor that reported an instantaneous current would sit at baseline almost always and catch a burst once in ~350 samples — and every charge we quote would have been a lower bound by a factor nobody had measured.
  • The two numbers that settle it, using no timestamps at all. 27 % of samples sit above baseline, not 0.3 %. And the largest single-sample excess ever recorded on that channel is 0.216 µA, where an instantaneous read of the same charge in 10 ms would have to read 10.1 µA — 47× larger. Turn that round: a sample that contains a whole burst and reads 0.216 µA must be averaging over at least Q/ΔI = 0.47 s. Both discriminate by two orders of magnitude, in the same direction.
  • And the trap we had to beat. The HV logger writes whole-second timestamps but its loop runs at 1.0162 s, so the true read time drifts through its labelled second and a raw-label fold is smeared by a 1 s box — which would fabricate a wide response from a narrow one. The drift is also the cure: the pattern of dropped seconds over-constrains the logger's own clock, and solving it recovers 95 % of the sample times to 2 ms. The sharp fold is what the figure shows.
